A coach works on you, over months, through a goal you've already named. A consultant does defined work for you and hands back an output. A diagnostician runs one session to find the constraint underneath a problem you've been describing wrong, then leaves. Coaching buys accountability. Consulting buys capacity. A diagnostic buys an accurate name for what's actually stuck. Most founders shopping for the first two need the third first.

That's the short answer. Below is how to tell which one your situation calls for.

## The three purchases

**A coach** takes a goal you can already state and holds you to it over time. The engagement is a relationship with a cadence — weekly or fortnightly, three to twelve months. The value compounds through repetition and accountability. This works when you know what you're trying to do and the gap is follow-through.

**A consultant** takes a defined scope and delivers an output: a strategy document, a hiring plan, a rebuilt funnel. You're buying execution capacity and domain expertise you don't want to build in-house. This works when you know what needs to happen and need someone to do it, or to do it better than you would.

**A diagnostician** takes the problem as you describe it and tests that description against the evidence. One session, one output: the constraint named in language you can act on. There's no ongoing relationship and no deliverable to implement. This works when you've been executing hard against your own explanation and the situation hasn't moved.

## The symptom that separates them

Coaching and consulting both assume your diagnosis is right. They operate downstream of it. A coach holds you to the goal you named; a consultant builds against the brief you wrote. Both are efficient once the diagnosis is sound, and both quietly multiply the error when it isn't.

So the question that separates the three purchases has nothing to do with budget or seniority. It's this: **how confident are you that you've correctly named what's wrong?**

Three tells that the diagnosis is the weak link:

- **Your explanation changes every quarter.** Same stuck situation, new theory each time. Last quarter it was hiring. This quarter it's positioning. The theory keeps updating; the situation doesn't.
- **You've executed a lot against it and nothing moved.** Real effort, real hours, no change in the outcome. That combination usually means the effort was pointed at a symptom.
- **Everyone around you sees something you don't feel yet.** Your team or your co-founder keeps circling one thing and it doesn't land for you.

If none of those are true, you probably need a coach or a consultant, and you should go buy one.

## The decision rule

| Your situation | What to buy |
|---|---|
| I know what's wrong and what to do. I don't do it consistently. | Coach |
| I know what's wrong and what to do. I lack the hands or the expertise. | Consultant |
| I've been sure what's wrong for months, I've worked hard at it, and it hasn't moved. | Diagnostic |
| I can't tell which of these I am. | Diagnostic — that uncertainty is the symptom |

## What a wrong diagnosis costs

A founder running an AI public-speaking platform came in certain his marketing was too slow. He wanted more content, more volume. Two questions in — *how many landing page views do you have?* and *how many people click the CTA?* — it turned out he didn't know either number, and that he'd had tracking wired into his site the whole time. He'd [never opened the dashboard](/case-studies/the-dashboard-he-never-opened).

Had he hired a consultant against his own brief, he'd have bought more marketing output on top of a system nobody was reading. The brief was the problem. A good consultant would have executed it faithfully.

The same shape shows up on the coaching side. [Minh at Algomate](/case-studies/algomate-10-hours-a-week) booked a session to narrow his niche. He wanted a framework for choosing. The first question was how many real customer conversations he'd had in six months, and the answer was two, maybe three. There was no niche to narrow — almost nobody had heard the pitch. Coaching him toward better niche discipline would have been months spent refining an answer to the wrong question. His words afterward: "Your question-asking approach helped me save 10 to 15 hours of nonsense outreach."

In both cases the founder was capable, working hard, and specific about the problem. The description was the expensive part.

## Why one session is the whole product

The obvious objection: if the diagnosis matters this much, shouldn't it take longer than ninety minutes?

It doesn't, because you already have the information. You've lived inside the situation for months. What's missing is a formulation you can act on, and that has to come from outside — from someone with no stake in your current explanation and no reason to be tactful about it. Your team can't do it; they report to you. Your investors can't; they're evaluating you. Your friends won't.

That's also why the engagement ends when the naming does. Founders who get the constraint named tend to move on their own, fast. What they were missing was the word for what to stop.

> "You know that way you already know something, but it hits when someone says it to you straight? Sometimes you just need to take your medicine."
>
> *Johny Harris, Dai Viet Rugby*

## Who should not book a diagnostic

Someone who wants validation for a decision they've already made. Someone at idea stage with no team, product, or customers yet — there's no system to find a constraint in. Someone who wants a long-term thinking partner, which is a real need and a coach's job.

And anyone who genuinely knows what's wrong. If you can state the constraint plainly and the evidence backs it, you don't need a diagnostician. You need to go execute, or hire someone to execute with you.
